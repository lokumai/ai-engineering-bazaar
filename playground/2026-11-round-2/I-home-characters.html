P1

Mimar Sinan and Nasreddin Hoca

Recommended

The great Ottoman architect, working a pair of dividers over a plan, and the folk teacher who explains anything with a story, one finger raised.

The clearest match of person to track, and both figures are warm, famous and peaceful.

  • Each figure names its track: Sinan is the engineer every reader in Turkey knows, Hoca is the teacher who makes a hard idea plain.
  • Both are known well beyond Turkey; UNESCO named 1996 the year of Nasreddin Hoca.
  • Sinan is a real person with a known face in miniatures; the drawing keeps to the white turban and grey beard, not a likeness.

P2

Hezarfen Ahmed Çelebi and a meddah

The inventor who is said to have flown from the Galata Tower, wearing his wings, and a meddah, the coffeehouse storyteller with his handkerchief and stick.

  • The wings make the engineer readable at a glance, and they move well.
  • The meddah is a Turkish tradition on UNESCO's intangible heritage list, and is a storyteller by trade.
  • The flight is a legend from one source (Evliya Çelebi); some readers will know that, most will not.

P3

A Janissary sapper and a Bazargan merchant

A Janissary from the corps that dug and built, in the tall white börk with its flap, carrying a lantern and a plan, and a merchant weighing goods on a brass balance.

  • The börk is one of the most recognisable Ottoman shapes, and the swinging flap and lantern give steady motion.
  • The merchant belongs in a bazaar more than anyone; the balance reads as "weighing things up fairly".
  • A Janissary is a soldier first; even with a lantern instead of a weapon, some readers see the army.

P4

A Sipahi and the Sadrazam

A Sipahi horseman in a pointed helmet with a falcon on his glove, and the grand vizier in the tall kallavi turban with its gold band, offering a scroll.

  • The two most courtly figures: rich costume, very Ottoman, very recognisable abroad.
  • Neither figure says engineer or plain reader; they stand for Ottoman history rather than for the tracks.
  • The falcon and the scroll keep both peaceful, but a cavalryman and a minister set a formal tone for a friendly front door.

P5

A coppersmith and a çay seller

Two people you still meet in a Turkish çarşı: a coppersmith hammering a pot, with a spark on each strike, and a tea seller with the hanging brass tray of tulip glasses.

  • The most bazaar-native pair, and the most continuous motion: the hammer and the swinging tray never stop.
  • No historical person is drawn, so no likeness to get right.
  • Less famous worldwide than Sinan or Hoca; they say "Turkey today" more than "Turkey's great names".
