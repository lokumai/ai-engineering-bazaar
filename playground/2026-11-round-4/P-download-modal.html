How does a reader pick what to download, and where does the download button sit?

The author asked for a download button in the catalog and in each module, but not in the navbar, opening a feature-rich modal like Canva's: pick modules or whole levels, pick the format (PDF or Markdown), then download. Below: where the button can sit, then three designs of what it opens. Every design works with the real course: 5 levels, 38 modules, of which 19 are written; each module has a For Engineers and a For Everyone track (none of the For Everyone modules is written yet) and three languages (English, Turkish, and Persian, which reads right to left). How the files are made, and how a file that mixes the two tracks is named, are on page Q.

Rules every design follows.
  • A module that is not written is listed but cannot be ticked, and says so in words (Planned, or Not written for everyone). A download never contains an empty file or a placeholder page.
  • A level checkbox has three states: all, some (a dash), none. It counts only the modules that can be downloaded.
  • The summary always says how many modules, about how large, and the exact file name, before the reader presses Download.
  • A download larger than 8 MB shows a preparing state with a progress bar, a percentage, a Cancel button and a status line a screen reader announces. Smaller ones start at once.
  • Keyboard: the dialog traps Tab, Escape closes it and returns focus to the button that opened it, every control has a label, and with reduced motion on nothing slides or pulses.
  • Persian: choosing it switches the preview to right to left, so the reader sees what they will get before they get it.
  • Try it: each frame below is live. Press the button on the page, press Escape, Tab around, or use the state buttons under the frame. The frame is a picture of the page, so the buttons in it are the ones the reader would press.

Spots Where the button goes

The navbar is ruled out. The red tags below mark every place that was considered, on the catalog and on a module page (which is already full: the contents rail on the left, the track and language switches in the bar, the table of contents on the right). Each design picks from this list.

  • C2 and M1 cost no space: C2 shares a row that is empty, M1 is one more item on the line that already says "30 min · 2,281 words".
  • C3 puts the download where the reader is looking at a level: it is the only spot that answers "this level" without opening a list first.
  • M4 is the quietest and the least found: it is at the very end of a 10,000-pixel page, so it only serves a reader who finished.
  • M5 exists only on a phone, where the rail and the table of contents fold into the Contents button.

P1 Two panes Recommended

The Canva shape. On the left, the whole course as a tree: a tri-state checkbox per level, a checkbox per module, sizes beside them. On the right, what the file will be: format as two cards, track, language, and a preview of the first page. A bar along the bottom says what will download, and holds the Download button. Spots: C2 and C3 on the catalog, M1 (and M5 on a phone) on a module.

Recommended: it is the closest to what the author described, it answers every question on one screen, and the preview is the only way to see Persian come out right to left before downloading.

  • Everything is visible at once, so nobody has to remember a choice from an earlier step. The reader who wants one module ticks one box; the reader who wants a level ticks one box.
  • Costs the most screen. At 1440 it is a 1000 px dialog; at 390 it becomes a full sheet, the right pane moves above the list, and the preview folds into a Preview line.
  • The module page opens it with the current module ticked, so the common case is one press and then Download.
  • The level icon on the catalog opens it with that level ticked.

P2 Three steps

A narrow dialog that asks one question at a time: what (this module, a level, chosen modules, or everything written), then the format with track and language, then a review with the file name and the Download button. Spots: C1 on the catalog, M3 and M4 on a module.

  • The clearest to read and the least to learn: each screen has one job, and the module page can skip step 1 because the answer is already known.
  • Slowest for the reader who knows what they want: three presses of Next to take one level.
  • Picking modules from several levels is a list inside step 1, so it is the same tree as P1, only hidden one choice deeper.
  • The preview shows only at the last step, so a wrong language is found late.

P3 Small popover, then the full list

The button opens a small popover for the common case: this module (or this level), PDF or Markdown, language, track, and a Download button. A line at the bottom, "More options", turns it into the full dialog with the tree, for the reader who wants several modules. Spots: M2 on a module, C3 on the catalog.

  • The fastest path for the most common request: two presses from a module page, and nothing else on screen to read.
  • Two surfaces to build and test instead of one: the popover and the full dialog (which here is a single column, without a preview).
  • No catalog button at the top: choosing modules from several levels is only reachable by opening a level's popover first, then More options. A reader who never meets the icon never finds it.
  • On a phone the popover is a sheet from the bottom edge.

Ask What the author decides here

Two decisions from this page; the other three are on page Q.

  • The modal: P1, P2 or P3. P1 and P3 can also be combined: P3's popover from the module page, P1's dialog for everything else.
  • Where the buttons go: any of C1 to C4 and M1 to M5, one or several. The module page has no navbar button in any design.
